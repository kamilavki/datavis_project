Introduction

Why This Question Matters

Over the past three decades, European countries have committed to shifting away from fossil fuels toward renewable energy. Political agreements, carbon targets, and green investment pledges have multiplied — but is the transition actually happening at the pace needed, and is it happening evenly across the continent?

This project explores energy mix data from 38 European countries between 1990 and 2024, combining Eurostat energy balance data with CO₂ emissions records from Our World in Data. The goal is to reveal who is leading the transition, who is falling behind, and what the aggregate numbers hide about individual country trajectories.

The dataset covers not just EU member states but also the UK, Norway, Iceland, the Western Balkans, Ukraine, Moldova and Georgia — giving a fuller picture of the broader European region and allowing meaningful comparisons between countries inside and outside the EU.

Data & Methods

Where the Data Comes From

Two public datasets were combined, cleaned, and merged on country and year to produce the final analytical dataset of 1,239 rows and 24 variables.

Eurostat · NRG_BAL_S

Simplified Energy Balances

Gross available energy by source for 38 European countries, 1990–2024. Measured in thousand tonnes of oil equivalent (ktoe). Covers electricity, heat, natural gas, oil, solid fossil fuels, renewables, nuclear, and more.

Our World in Data · GitHub

CO₂ & GHG Emissions

Production-based CO₂ emissions, per capita figures, total greenhouse gas data, GDP, and population for all countries worldwide. Used to add emissions context to the energy mix data.

Limitations: GDP data is missing for ~5.8% of rows. Bosnia and Herzegovina only has energy data from 2014 onward. Consumption-based CO₂ (which accounts for imported emissions) is not available for all countries. The Eurostat dataset covers gross available energy, which includes imports and excludes energy used for transformation — this affects comparability with production-side emissions figures.

Ethics & Representation

White Hat vs Black Hat

The same dataset can tell very different stories depending on how it is presented. The white-hat visualization shows Europe's genuine progress on emissions reduction. The black-hat visualization uses the same data to expose how that progress can be misleading — by showing absolute emissions, outsourced pollution, and the gap between pledges and reality.

⚪ White Hat — The Progress Story
⚫ Black Hat — The Hidden Reality

Note: the white-hat perspective highlights countries making genuine progress. The black-hat perspective reveals how aggregate EU statistics can mask countries that are increasing emissions or outsourcing their carbon footprint through imports.

Conclusion

Key Findings

The data reveals a deeply uneven energy transition across Europe. Progress is real but unevenly distributed, and aggregate statistics can obscure significant variation between countries.

🌿

Clear Leaders

Iceland (83.6%), Norway (56.7%) and Sweden (48.4%) lead on renewable share in 2024, driven by hydropower, wind and strong policy frameworks.

⛽

Persistent Laggards

Malta (94.9%), the Netherlands (85%) and Cyprus (86.7%) remain overwhelmingly fossil-dependent in 2024, with slow transition progress.

📉

Germany's Progress

Germany cut CO₂ per capita from 13.2t in 1990 to 6.8t in 2024 — a 49% reduction — but still relies heavily on oil and gas.

🔄

Outsourced Emissions

Countries like Luxembourg and Belgium consume far more CO₂ than they produce — effectively exporting their emissions to manufacturing countries.

📊

Renewables Work

Across all 38 countries in 2024, renewable share and CO₂ per capita show a strong negative correlation (r = −0.52) — more renewables means less CO₂.

⚠️

Pace Too Slow

Despite progress, the EU's own 2030 climate targets require significantly faster reductions than current trajectories suggest are likely.
